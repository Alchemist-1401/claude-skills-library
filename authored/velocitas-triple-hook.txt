# Velocitas triple-hook writing

Create openings that make an F1 fan understand the subject, want an answer, and reconsider their first expectation. Then deliver the answer. This is an original Velocitas adaptation of the three-part method explained in the owner's Kallaway reference. It is not a creator-supplied skill or scientifically proven formula. See the Source and limits section below for provenance.

## Intake without making the owner repeat context

Read the current Velocitas brief and relevant supplied material first. Establish:

1. The single F1 idea or event and who should care.
2. The actual answer, explanation or payoff.
3. What is verified, what is an inference, and what is only an illustrative example.
4. Available original or licensed assets and any existing length or format requirement.

Ask only for a missing fact that prevents an honest draft. If the owner wants ideas rather than a real race report, use an explicitly illustrative scenario. Do not attach a hypothetical result to a real driver, team or Grand Prix.

For current standings, results, rules, dates, penalties, statistics or quotations, inspect an authoritative current source before using them. Save each claim's source URL and date in the working draft. Distinguish an official finding, a reported claim and your analysis. Never invent telemetry, team radio, a quote or a precise numerical advantage. If research access is unavailable, use supplied evidence or leave a visible verification gap. Do not write an unverified fact into a finished hook.

## The three moves

### 1. Context: let the right viewer recognize the subject immediately

The first image and opening words should identify the F1 situation. Use concrete nouns and a visible event, not a vague promise such as 'You won't believe this'. Avoid a logo intro or a long setup. Put necessary context early in the first sentence rather than hiding it at the end.

The screen, narration and optional sound should reinforce one idea. The hook must still be understandable with sound off through readable text or clear visual action. Do not make comprehension depend on a caption below the post.

### 2. Lean: give the viewer a reason to want the next beat

Choose one honest reason to care:

- A consequence a fan understands: position, tyres, strategy, a missed opportunity.
- A real question: why did a driver pit, lift, defend or lose time?
- A supported contrast, unusual observation or unresolved decision.
- A relatable fan frustration, with a useful explanation coming.

Curiosity must come from the subject, not fake urgency or an unsupported number. No invented '99% of fans miss this', fake expertise, fake audience stats or fabricated 'secret'.

### 3. Snapback: change the expectation, not the truth

Name the plausible first explanation, then turn toward the supported explanation. The contrast must matter to the story. It can reveal that the obvious advantage was a disadvantage, the apparent cause was not the decisive one, or the key moment happened earlier than expected.

Do not invent a contrarian position to satisfy the pattern. If evidence supports only a straightforward explanation, say so and use context plus curiosity without forcing a snapback. Correctness beats formula compliance.

The turn must not contradict the eventual explanation, conceal a decisive qualification, accuse someone without evidence or promise a result the Reel never shows.

## Workflow

1. Write the payoff in one plain sentence before writing the hook. If you cannot state the answer, you are not ready to promise it.
2. Draft three meaningfully different openings: a consequence-led one, a question-led one, and a supported expectation-reversal one. Do not produce a long list of near-identical synonyms.
3. For each candidate, specify the first-frame visual, on-screen text, spoken line if used, curiosity question and exact payoff beat. Read the Original Velocitas examples section below for illustrative patterns, not facts to reuse as race news.
4. Reject any candidate with unclear context, an unverified claim, a forced reversal or a missing payoff. Pick the strongest remaining candidate and briefly explain why. If none passes, fix the premise before polishing the wording.
5. Map the selected opening into a short beat sequence: context -> lean -> snapback where justified -> evidence/explanation -> payoff -> optional relevant invitation. The hook is not permission to pad the rest of the video.
6. Hand the opening and beat map to the existing Velocitas production workflow. Use the runtime the idea needs, not a universal 7-, 15- or 30-second rule. Do not stretch a small answer into a long wait. Do not cut a necessary explanation merely to hit a duration target.
7. Review the actual rendered opening at phone size if production is requested and available. Check text wrapping, pacing, visual focus, source labels and sound-off comprehension. A plan or a successful export is not a viewed result. If you cannot render or inspect it, label the output as an unrendered draft.
8. Return one selected opening, the payoff plan, verification notes and any real blocker. Stop before publishing.

## Output contract

For a writing request, return:

- **Selected opening:** first-frame visual, short on-screen line, optional spoken line.
- **Why it earns the next beat:** context, lean, and snapback if one is justified.
- **Payoff map:** a compact sequence showing exactly where the viewer gets the promised answer.
- **Truth and rights check:** sources for load-bearing claims; hypothetical or reconstructed material labeled; asset limitations.
- **Optional next action:** a relevant discussion question, share reason or follow promise, only when the content earns it.

Default to one selected draft, with two short alternatives only if comparison helps the owner's decision. Do not expose a long internal scoring exercise.

For a carousel request, adapt rather than pretending a static card has narration: slide 1 gives context and a useful question; slide 2 turns the expectation if justified; subsequent slides show evidence and pay off the opening. Keep the answer within the carousel. A cover must remain intelligible without hidden slide notes.

## Quality gate

Every finished draft must pass:

- A relevant F1 viewer can tell what this is about from the opening image/text.
- There is one clear reason to keep watching or swipe.
- Any reversal is supported and does not mislead.
- The promised answer is actually delivered, not deferred to a follow, comment or DM.
- Numbers, quotes, rules, race results and causal claims have evidence or are left out.
- Humorous exaggeration is plainly a joke, not disguised reporting.
- No fake view counters, analytics, income, testimonials or audience reactions.
- No copied creator wording, logo, footage or third-party assets without rights.
- No black background or unapproved rebrand imposed on Velocitas.
- No paid service or credit requirement quietly added.
- No account action, posting or growth guarantee.

## CTA and testing discipline

Prefer an earned invitation tied to the subject, such as asking which strategy choice the viewer would make. Do not copy 'comment hooks' or 'DM HIT' from the references. Do not promise a resource that does not exist. A follow promise should describe repeatable value the page can deliver, not 'follow for more' without a reason.

Treat this method as a testable writing technique. If authorized account insights are supplied, compare similar posts using available early retention, average watch time/completion, shares per reach and follows per reach. Label unavailable metrics and avoid claiming causation from one post. Keyword comments from a free-resource CTA are not proof of loyal followers or genuine discussion. No baseline or result should be invented.


# Original Velocitas examples

These are illustrative teaching scenarios, not reports of real races or approved posts. Verify the mechanism and any event-specific claims before production. Use neutral original car/track graphics, not fabricated footage of a real driver.

## Example 1: the undercut

**Premise:** a simplified two-car race: the trailing car pits for fresh tyres; its gain on the next lap is enough to move it ahead after the other car stops. Real outcomes depend on tyre warm-up, traffic, pit losses and pace.

- Context / visual: a leader and a trailing car approach pit entry. The trailing car turns in.
- On-screen opening: 'Why is the leader worried?'
- Spoken context: 'The car behind just pitted.'
- Lean: 'That early stop could put it ahead.'
- Snapback: 'The next pass might happen in the pit lane, not at a corner.'
- Payoff: animate the two routes and the fresh-tyre out-lap; show their rejoin order. Explain that the undercut uses time gained on fresh tyres to jump a rival during the pit-stop sequence.
- Qualification: do not imply pitting first always wins. Show when this scenario's assumptions hold, then name traffic and tyre warm-up as reasons it can fail.
- Relevant invitation: 'Would you cover the stop or stay out?'

Why this works as a draft: the viewer understands the event, anticipates an overtake, then learns why track position can change without a conventional passing move. If the diagram does not clearly demonstrate that, rewrite or remove the hook.

## Example 2: a fan myth explainer

**Topic:** whether a softer tyre always makes a car faster over a stint.

- Context: a clear tyre comparison with no real-team performance numbers.
- Opening: 'Softer tyres. Faster race?'
- Lean: 'One lap can make that look obvious.'
- Snapback: 'The tyre that wins a lap can still lose the stint.'
- Payoff: show a qualitative pace-over-laps sketch, clearly labeled illustrative. Explain the trade-off between initial pace and degradation, with current technical sources checked before recording.
- Qualification: no made-up crossover lap, no universal compound rule, no claim that the sketch is actual telemetry.

## Example 3: when to reject the pattern

Weak idea: 'This driver secretly lost on purpose. But nobody noticed.'

Reject it. An attention-grabbing reversal does not justify an allegation. If the evidence is only a visible mistake, explain that mistake with the limits of the footage. Do not turn speculation about motive into a hook.

Weak idea: '99% of F1 fans get this rule wrong.'

Reject the invented percentage. Name the rule and the understandable misconception directly, then cite its current wording and show a worked example.

## Carousel adaptation

Using the illustrative undercut scenario:

1. Cover: 'Why is the leader worried?' with the trailing car turning into pit entry.
2. Prediction: 'He is behind. He stops first. Does that help the leader?'
3. Turn: show how a fresh-tyre lap can change the time gap.
4. Payoff: show the rejoin, explain the undercut, and label the simplified assumptions.
5. Discussion if useful: ask which response the reader would choose, with both strategic options grounded in the scenario.

Do not hide the explanation behind a comment keyword or promise a free guide that has not been made.
